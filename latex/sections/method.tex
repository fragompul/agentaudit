\section{Preliminaries and Methodology}\label{sec:method}
\paragraph{Setting and estimand.} Agents $m=1,\dots,M$ are evaluated on tasks $t=1,\dots,T$ with $R$ runs each, giving scores $Y_{mtr}\in[0,1]$ (binary in our benchmark). We regard tasks as an i.i.d.\ sample from a task population and runs as conditionally independent given agent and task. The estimand of an agent is the population benchmark score $\mu_m=E_t[p_{mt}]$, where $p_{mt}=E[Y_{mtr}\mid m,t]$. Agents are treated as \emph{fixed}: the $M$ evaluated systems are not a sample from a population of agents, so the agent component below describes the spread of these $M$ systems rather than a population variance. Section~\ref{sec:limits} states what this does and does not license.

\subsection{pass@$k$ and pass$^k$}
With $c$ successes in $n$ runs of one task, $\binom{c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}$ is an unbiased estimator of $p^k$ (all $k$ runs succeed) and $1-\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}$ is unbiased for $1-(1-p)^k$ for $k\le n$ \citep{chen2021codex}; unbiasedness is verified by exact summation over the binomial law in the test suite. We report benchmark-level means of these per-task estimators; the plug-in $\hat p^{\,k}$ is biased upwards. $k>n$ is refused rather than extrapolated. pass$^k$ is the reliability metric of $\tau$-bench \citep{yao2024taubench}; it decreases in $k$ whereas pass@$k$ increases.

\subsection{Intervals for a benchmark score}\label{sec:intervals}
Let $p_t$ be the success probability of task $t$, with mean $p$ and variance $\tau^2$ across tasks. The variance of the benchmark mean is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(\bar Y)=\frac{\tau^2}{T}+\frac{p(1-p)-\tau^2}{TR},
\end{equation}
while a binomial interval on the pooled $TR$ runs assumes $p(1-p)/(TR)$. Their ratio is the design effect \citep{kish1965survey}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{DEFF}=1+(R-1)\rho,\qquad \rho=\tau^2/\{p(1-p)\},
\end{equation}
so a nominal 95\% pooled interval has asymptotic coverage $2\Phi(1.96/\sqrt{\mathrm{DEFF}})-1$. We implement Wilson \citep{wilson1927} and Clopper--Pearson \citep{clopper1934} intervals (valid for one task or i.i.d.\ runs), the \emph{task bootstrap} (resample whole tasks; percentile and BCa \citep{efron1987bca,efron1993bootstrap}), a \emph{runs-only} bootstrap (ignores $\tau^2/T$), a \emph{two-stage} bootstrap (tasks, then runs; counts run noise twice), and an overdispersed beta-binomial profile-likelihood interval for the mean of $p_t\sim\mathrm{Beta}(\mu\kappa,(1-\mu)\kappa)$.

\subsection{Variance decomposition}
We fit the crossed random-effects model $Y_{mtr}=\mu+a_m+b_t+(ab)_{mt}+e_{mtr}$ with variances $\sigma^2_m,\sigma^2_t,\sigma^2_{mt},\sigma^2_e$ by the ANOVA method of moments (expected mean squares in Appendix~\ref{app:ems}), in the spirit of generalizability theory \citep{cronbach1972,brennan2001}. Negative estimates are truncated at zero and the raw values are kept. For binary scores this is a linear-probability decomposition: exact for variances of means, not a latent-scale model. With $R=1$ the interaction and run error are confounded and the library flags it. Two derived quantities matter in practice: the variance of the difference between two agents,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Var}(\bar Y_a-\bar Y_b)=2\Big(\frac{\sigma^2_{mt}}{T}+\frac{\sigma^2_e}{TR}\Big),
\end{equation}
in which the task main effect cancels, and the generalizability coefficient $E\rho^2=\sigma^2_m/(\sigma^2_m+\sigma^2_{mt}/T+\sigma^2_e/(TR))$.

\subsection{Paired comparison and multiplicity}
For agents $a,b$ let $d_t=\bar Y_{at}-\bar Y_{bt}$; the task is the unit of analysis. We provide (i) a sign-flip permutation test of $\sum_t d_t$, (ii) a studentised sign-flip test of the paired $t$ statistic, (iii) a cluster bootstrap for the mean difference, (iv) exact McNemar \citep{mcnemar1947}, (v) Cohen's $d_z$ and the probability of superiority $P(d>0)+\tfrac12P(d=0)$. The plain sign-flip test is exact (conditionally on $|d_t|$) only under the \emph{strong} null that each $d_t$ is symmetric about zero, which holds if the two agents are exchangeable within each task; it is not exact for the weak null $E[d]=0$ with skewed $d$ \citep{romano1990randomization}, for which the studentised version is asymptotically valid by analogy with the studentised permutation theory of \citet{janssen1997studentized}, who treats the two-sample problem; we did not prove the one-sample result and check it by simulation (E1). It is enumerated exactly up to 14 non-zero differences; beyond that it is Monte Carlo with $n_\text{perm}=20{,}000$ seeded draws and p-value $(1+\#\{|S^*|\ge|S|\})/(n_\text{perm}+1)$. McNemar's test needs one binary outcome per task: for $R=1$ it is exact and conditional on the discordant pairs; for $R>1$ we use the per-task majority outcome (strictly more than half of the runs; a task with exactly half is scored as a failure, an arbitrary convention). This changes the estimand to the difference of majority-success rates and discards the run-level information; it is a sensitivity analysis, not a conservative version of the primary test (Appendix~\ref{app:taubench} shows cases where it is less and more significant than the mean-score analysis, depending on how ties are treated). Pairwise p-values are adjusted by Holm \citep{holm1979} (family-wise error under any dependence) or Benjamini--Hochberg \citep{benjamini1995} (false discovery rate under independence or positive dependence \citep{benjamini2001dependency}; pairwise tests sharing agents are dependent, so Holm is our default).

\paragraph{What a non-significant difference means.} Failing to reject $H_0:\mu_a=\mu_b$ means a difference was not \emph{detected} at this sample size. It is not evidence of equality. Equivalence is claimed only when a confidence interval or sequence lies inside a pre-declared margin \citep{lakens2017equivalence}; ``not distinguishable'' in this paper never means ``equivalent''.

\subsection{Ranking uncertainty}
We resample tasks (the same tasks for every agent), recompute scores and ranks (ties broken uniformly at random) and report rank distributions, $P(\text{best})$, the matrix of $P(\bar Y_i>\bar Y_j)$ and the fraction of pairs that cannot be told apart. A split-half Kendall $\tau$ measures whether a fresh task sample would reproduce the ordering. Wide rank intervals show ranking uncertainty; they do not show that agents are equivalent.

\subsection{Power and the tasks-versus-repeats trade-off}\label{sec:power}
Let $s_t=2\sigma^2_{mt}$ and $s_e=2\sigma^2_e$. The mean paired difference has $\mathrm{Var}=(s_t+s_e/R)/T$. At a fixed number of runs $B=TR$ this equals $(Rs_t+s_e)/B$, which is increasing in $R$ whenever $s_t>0$: if tasks are free, a fixed number of runs is best spent on more tasks, and the advantage grows with the interaction $\sigma^2_{mt}$ (it vanishes as $s_t\to0$). With cost $c_\text{task}$ per new task and $c_\text{run}$ per run, minimising the variance at budget $T(c_\text{task}+Rc_\text{run})$ gives
\begin{equation}
R^\star=\sqrt{\frac{s_e\,c_\text{task}}{s_t\,c_\text{run}}}.
\end{equation}
Which cost scenario applies matters: a budget counted in runs, in money (tasks may need environment setup) or in wall-clock time (runs may share a machine) corresponds to different $c_\text{task}/c_\text{run}$. Power uses the noncentral $t$ distribution with $T-1$ degrees of freedom and the planner reports the minimum detectable effect. The variance components are themselves estimated, so tasks-needed and detectable-effect figures computed from a pilot are plug-in values with substantial uncertainty and should be read as orders of magnitude. Repeats remain essential for reliability metrics and for estimating $\sigma^2_e$.

\subsection{Anytime-valid sequential evaluation}
\paragraph{Construction.} For per-task quantities $X_t=(d_t+1)/2\in[0,1]$ with mean $m$, define for each candidate mean $m$ the capital processes $K^{\pm}_t(m)=\prod_{i\le t}\{1\pm\lambda_i^{\pm}(m)(X_i-m)\}$ and the hedged process $K_t(m)=\frac12\{K^+_t(m)+K^-_t(m)\}$. The bets are the predictable plug-in bets of \citet{waudbysmith2024betting}, $\lambda_i=\sqrt{2\log(2/\alpha)/(\hat\sigma^2_{i-1}\,i\log(1+i))}$, truncated at $\lambda_i^+(m)=\min(\lambda_i,\frac12/m)$ and $\lambda_i^-(m)=\min(\lambda_i,\frac12/(1-m))$; $\hat\sigma^2_{i-1}$ uses $X_1,\dots,X_{i-1}$ only. Because every factor is at least $1/2$, both processes are nonnegative; if the $X_i$ are i.i.d.\ with mean $m$ they are martingales with initial value one (verified by exact enumeration over three-point laws in the test suite), hence so is $K_t(m)$. By Ville's inequality $P(\sup_t K_t(m)\ge 1/\alpha)\le\alpha$. The set $C_t=\bigcap_{s\le t}\{m:K_s(m)<1/\alpha\}$ therefore contains the true mean at all times simultaneously with probability at least $1-\alpha$, so it may be inspected after every task and the experiment stopped at any data-dependent time \citep{howard2021cs,ramdas2023gametheoretic}. We report the hull $[\ell_t,u_t]$ of the retained candidates on a grid of 2001 means, widened by one grid cell, with $\ell_t$ non-decreasing and $u_t$ non-increasing. The hull is a superset of $C_t$; the widening guards against the grid, but the guarantee for means lying between grid points is numerical (resolution $5\times10^{-4}$ in $X$), not a proof. We verified bound invariance under a 20-fold finer grid (test suite).

\paragraph{Assumptions.} The guarantee is for the mean of the task distribution: tasks must be i.i.d.\ draws from it and the $X_t$ bounded in $[0,1]$ (the per-task difference of $R$-run means lies in $[-1,1]$ for any $R$). Evaluating a fixed finite pool in random order is sampling without replacement; the guarantee then refers to the population mean only if the pool is regarded as an i.i.d.\ sample, which is the same assumption as in the rest of the paper. Nothing is assumed about the distribution of $X_t$ beyond boundedness, and the run noise within a task is absorbed in $X_t$.

\paragraph{Decision rule and what it guarantees.} Let $\Delta$ be the true mean paired difference, $\varepsilon\ge0$ the margin and $[\ell_t,u_t]$ the interval for $\Delta$ obtained by mapping $C_t$ back to the difference scale. From task $t\ge t_{\min}$ the procedure stops with \emph{$a$ better} if $\ell_t>\varepsilon$, with \emph{$b$ better} if $u_t<-\varepsilon$, and with \emph{equivalent} if $-\varepsilon\le\ell_t$ and $u_t\le\varepsilon$ (only possible for $\varepsilon>0$, and only if the \emph{whole} sequence, not the point estimate, lies inside the band). It stays \emph{undecided} after the last task otherwise. Each wrong decision requires $\Delta
otin[\ell_t,u_t]$ at the stopping time: a wrong ``$a$ better'' means $\Delta\le\varepsilon<\ell_t$; a wrong ``$b$ better'' means $\Delta\ge-\varepsilon>u_t$; a wrong ``equivalent'' means $|\Delta|>\varepsilon$ while $[\ell_t,u_t]\subseteq[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$. All three are contained in the single event that the confidence sequence misses $\Delta$ at some time, so the probability of \emph{any} wrong decision, of any of the three kinds, is at most $\alpha$ (not $2\alpha$ as in two one-sided tests), under optional stopping. This is a theoretical bound under the assumptions above; it is neither a guarantee of power nor of a specific rate such as ``at most $x\%$'' for a given $\Delta$: the actual frequency at a given $\Delta$ can be far below $\alpha$ and is reported in Section~\ref{sec:sim} with Monte Carlo intervals. When $\Delta=0$ and $\varepsilon=0$ the procedure can never declare equivalence and ends undecided with probability near one minus the false-positive rate; when $|\Delta|\le\varepsilon$ the sequence reaches ``equivalent'' only once its width is below $2\varepsilon$ (sample size grows like $\varepsilon^{-2}$), so small margins are unreachable at benchmark sizes; when $\Delta$ lies just outside the band, ``equivalent'' remains possible with probability at most $\alpha$.
