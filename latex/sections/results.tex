\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Simulation: validating the statistics (RQ1, RQ2, RQ4)}\label{sec:sim}
\paragraph{Interval coverage (RQ1).} Figure~\ref{fig:coverage} shows coverage of nominal 95\% intervals for the benchmark score ($\sigma_b=1.5$, interaction sd $0.5$). With one run per task the pooled binomial intervals are fine (they are exact in that case). With three runs they cover \covWilsonThreeRuns{} of the time and with ten runs only \covWilsonTenRuns{} (100 tasks; Monte Carlo s.e.\ \covWilsonTenRunsSE{} points), as the design effect predicts. A runs-only bootstrap is worse (\covRunsTenRuns). The task bootstrap, its BCa variant and the hierarchical beta-binomial interval stay close to nominal (task bootstrap: \covBootTenRuns{} at ten runs; BCa \covBCaTenRuns; beta-binomial \covBBTenRuns), and the two-stage bootstrap is conservative (\covTwoTenRuns). Coverage of the task bootstrap for pass$^k$ ranges between \covPassKMin{} and \covPassKMax{}. Full numbers are in Appendix~\ref{app:tables}.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/coverage.pdf}
\caption{Coverage of nominal 95\% intervals for the benchmark score as runs per task grow (task sd $1.5$, interaction sd $0.5$, true score $\approx 0.43$). Pooling all runs breaks down already at three runs.}\label{fig:coverage}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Robustness of coverage (E3).} The ranking of methods holds across prevalence and task heterogeneity, but the task bootstrap is not uniformly near nominal (Figure~\ref{fig:covrob}). For $T\ge50$ and $R>1$ it covers \covrobBootMin{}--\covrobBootMax{} and the beta-binomial interval \covrobBBMin{}--\covrobBBMax{}. With $T=20$, one run per task and extreme prevalence with homogeneous tasks the percentile bootstrap covers only \covrobBootSmallMin{}--\covrobBootSmallMax{} (BCa: from \covrobBCaSmallMin{}) because the resampled score is nearly degenerate; the pooled Wilson interval is valid there and should be preferred. The runs-only bootstrap has zero coverage at $R=1$ (\covrobRunsOnlyOne{}) because it sees no variation. The pooled Wilson interval fails only when runs are clustered \emph{and} tasks differ: at ten runs and task sd $1.5$ it covers \covrobWilsonMin{}--\covrobWilsonMax{}, and it is roughly nominal when tasks are nearly homogeneous. The grid is small ($T\in\{20,50,100\}$, $R\in\{1,3,10\}$, three prevalences, two levels of task heterogeneity, 1000 replications per cell, Monte Carlo s.e.\ up to 1.6 points), so we do not derive a sample-size threshold from it. What it supports is conditional: where runs are clustered within tasks the pooled interval should not be used, task-level intervals are the better default but are mildly optimistic even at $T\ge50$ (coverage as low as \covrobBootMin{}), and for very small single-run evaluations an exact binomial interval is preferable.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/coverage_robustness.pdf}
\caption{Coverage at ten runs per task for two task-heterogeneity levels and three prevalences (marker shape). Pooled Wilson loses coverage when tasks differ; the task-level methods approach the nominal level as $T$ grows.}\label{fig:covrob}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Error rates of paired tests (RQ1).} With agent-by-task interaction sd $=1$, $T=100$ and ten runs per task, tests that treat each run as an independent pair reject a true null in \typeIPoolZ{} (two-proportion $z$-test) and \typeIPoolMc{} (run-level McNemar; Monte Carlo s.e.\ \typeIPoolMcSE{} points) of simulations, whereas the task-level sign-flip and bootstrap tests reject in \typeIPerm{} and \typeIBoot{} (Figure~\ref{fig:typeI}). With smaller interaction (sd $0.5$) run-level McNemar is still liberal (\typeIPoolMcLow). The pooled $z$-test is not always anti-conservative (it ignores pairing and is conservative at small $R$), so the harm of pooling is a loss of validity rather than a uniform inflation. Across all cells with a true null the sign-flip test rejects between \typeIPermMin{} and \typeIPermMax{}.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/typeI.pdf}
\caption{Type-I error at $\alpha=0.05$ for $T=100$ tasks and two agents of equal skill.}\label{fig:typeI}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Weak null with skewed differences (E1).} The simulation above has symmetric per-task differences, where the sign-flip test is exact. We also generated paired differences with mean zero but heavy skewness (a rare large win offset by frequent small losses; skewness up to $5.5$). No procedure exceeded the pre-specified tolerance of 2 points over 5\% at $T\ge100$ (Figure~\ref{fig:asym}): across all cells the largest rejection rates were \asymMaxSF{} for the sign-flip test, \asymMaxSSF{} for its studentised version, \asymMaxBoot{} for the paired bootstrap and \asymMaxT{} for the paired $t$-test, with Monte Carlo error of about 0.6 points. The sign-flip tests are conservative on average (mean rejection \asymMeanSF{}); the bootstrap and $t$ tests are on average at nominal (\asymMeanBoot{}) but occasionally 1--2 points above it. We therefore keep the sign-flip test as the default, but we state its exactness only for symmetric differences.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/asymmetric_null.pdf}
\caption{Type-I error at $\alpha=0.05$ with $R=5$ for paired differences with mean zero and increasing skewness (left to right).}\label{fig:asym}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Tasks or repeats? (RQ2, E2).} In the original configuration (task sd $1.5$, interaction sd $0.6$) the standard error of the score difference at a fixed budget of \designBudget{} runs grows by a factor of \designSEratio{} between one and twenty runs per task, and power falls from \designPowerOne{} to \designPowerTen{} (ten runs) and \designPowerTwenty{} (twenty runs) (Figure~\ref{fig:design}); the analytic variance model predicts the simulated standard errors to within \designMaxSEgap{}. This is not a general law. Across nine configurations (E2, Figure~\ref{fig:alloc}) the standard-error ratio of ten versus one run per task is \allocRatioLow{} when the interaction sd is $0.2$ (repeats cost nothing) and \allocRatioHigh{} when it is $1.0$; the task standard deviation hardly matters, because the task main effect cancels in a paired difference. The cost-optimal number of runs per task for a task that costs ten runs is \allocRstarTenLow{} with small interaction and \allocRstarTenHigh{} with large interaction. If the pool of tasks is capped (for example at 120), the question changes and the extra budget must go to repeats. Analytic and simulated standard errors agree to within \allocMaxSEgap{} in E2. Repeats are not useless: they identify run noise and enable pass$^k$.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/design.pdf}
\caption{Fixed budget $B=1200$ runs, $T=B/R$ tasks. Left: standard error of the difference between two agents; right: power of the paired $t$-test.}\label{fig:design}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Sequential evaluation (RQ4).} Applying a fixed-sample test after every task (``peeking'') and stopping at the first $p<0.05$ gives \seqNullPeek{} false positives under the null, against \seqNullSeq{} for the confidence sequence and \seqNullFixed{} for the fixed-$n$ test (Figure~\ref{fig:seq}; 600 replications per cell, Monte Carlo s.e.\ up to 2 points). The price is power: with a true difference of \seqMidDiff{} points the sequence decides in \seqPowerSeqMid{} of runs against \seqPowerFixedMid{} for the fixed-$n$ test at the full budget of \seqBudget{} tasks. When the effect is large it stops early (median \seqLargeMedianTasks{} tasks for the largest difference). Confidence sequences buy the right to stop at any time; they do not make evaluation cheaper at a given power.
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/sequential.pdf}
\caption{Sequential comparison, $R=3$ runs per task. Left: fraction declared significant (the line marks 5\%). Right: median number of tasks used when the sequence stops.}\label{fig:seq}
\end{figure}
Equivalence is only possible with a margin (E5, \seqmReps{} replications per cell). With margin zero ``equivalent'' was never declared (\seqmZeroMarginEquiv) and, for two agents of identical skill, the sequence stayed undecided in \seqmUndecidedTie{} of runs within 300 tasks: absence of a decision is not equivalence. With a margin of 10 points and a true gap of zero, equivalence was declared in \seqmEquivTrueTie{} of runs (median \seqmEquivTrueTieTasks{} tasks). When the true gap exceeded the margin, equivalence was declared in at most \seqmFalseEquivMax{} of runs per cell (95\% Clopper--Pearson upper limit \seqmFalseEquivUpper, \seqmReps{} replications); this is a simulation frequency in the cells we ran, far below the bound $\alpha=5\%$ that the theory gives for any bounded i.i.d.\ law (Section~\ref{sec:method}), and it is not itself a guarantee for other laws. A second simulation with a three-point law for the per-task difference (Appendix~\ref{app:equiv}, \equivReps{} replications per row) found no wrong decision rate above \equivWrongMax{} and a confidence-sequence miss rate of at most \equivMissMax{}, i.e.\ no violation of the 5\% bound, so in these scenarios the sequence was conservative (the observed miss rate is well below $\alpha$; a low observed rate does not itself establish validity, which rests on the argument of Section~\ref{sec:method}). Reaching ``equivalent'' depends strongly on how dispersed the task differences are: with a margin of 10 points and a true gap of zero it was declared in \seqmEquivTrueTie{} of runs in the main simulation but in only \equivTieMTen{} of runs when the per-task differences are three-point and more dispersed. Narrow margins are rarely reachable with 300 tasks.

\paragraph{Ranking uncertainty and near ties (RQ3, E6).} Bootstrap rank intervals are conservative for the population ranking (coverage \rankCovMin--\rankCovMax{} for nominal 95\%), and $P(\mathrm{best})$ is reasonably calibrated, noisily at $T=50$ (Figure~\ref{fig:rankcal}). In a six-agent leaderboard with one exactly tied pair and one pair \rankpwCloseTrue{} points apart, Holm-adjusted tests rejected the tied pair in at most \rankpwFWE{} of replications in every cell (the family-wise error is controlled and the procedure is conservative), but separated the close pair in only \rankpwCloseRej{} of replications even with 500 tasks and three runs per task (and \rankpwCloseRejSmall{} with 50 tasks). Small gaps are, in practice, not resolved by realistic benchmark sizes.

\subsection{Re-analysis of public results (RQ3)}\label{sec:public}
\paragraph{SWE-bench Verified.} The \sweNAgents{} entries yield \sweNPairs{} agent pairs. After Holm adjustment \sweFracHolm{} are distinguishable at $\alpha=0.05$ (\sweFracBH{} with Benjamini--Hochberg; \sweFracRaw{} without correction), so \sweIndistHolm{} cannot be separated; ``cannot be separated'' means not detected, not equal. The result is driven by the score gap (Figure~\ref{fig:swe}): pairs 2--5 points apart are separable in \sweGapSmallHolm{} of cases after correction, pairs 5--10 points apart in \sweGapMidHolm{}, and larger gaps almost always. The smallest distinguishable gap is \sweMinGap{} points and the median is \sweMedGap. The top entry (\sweTopScore) has a 95\% rank interval of 1--\sweTopRankHi{} and probability \sweTopPBest{} of being best.

\paragraph{Sensitivity to undocumented evaluation details.} The headline count of adjacent positions depends on the entries retained (Table~\ref{tab:swerobust}). Among all entries, \sweAllAdjHolm{} of adjacent leaderboard positions are distinguishable after Holm over \emph{all} pairs (\sweAllAdjRaw{} without correction). Restricting to entries whose coverage of the 500 instances could be verified gives \sweCovAdjHolm{}; restricting to entries that declare a single attempt (\sweNSingle{} entries) gives \sweSingleAdjHolm{}, presumably because fewer entries leave larger gaps between neighbours. The share of separable pairs is stable (\sweCovFracHolm{} coverage-verified, \sweSingleFracHolm{} single-attempt, \sweBothFracHolm{} both). The qualitative conclusions, that most pairs but almost no adjacent positions are separable, therefore hold across the subsets, whereas the earlier claim that \emph{none} of the adjacent positions is distinguishable is not robust: it is true only for the full set. The ``coverage verified'' label is weaker than it sounds: it means that public log folders plus the \texttt{no\_generation} list account for all 500 instances, and \sweNFolderCovWithNoLogs{} such entries nonetheless list instances under \texttt{no\_logs} (instances without an evaluation log, which the official convention also scores as unresolved). Overall \sweNNoLogs{} of the \sweNAgents{} entries have at least one such instance (up to \sweMaxNoLogs{}); \texttt{no\_generation} instances are genuine failures of the system, \texttt{no\_logs} instances are unobserved. Appendix~\ref{app:swemissing} therefore repeats the analysis on the \sweCompleteN{} entries without missing logs (\sweCompleteFracHolm{} of pairs separable), with pairwise complete cases (\sweCCFracHolm) and with the least favourable filling of every missing log (\sweRobustFracHolm): the share of separable pairs moves by less than two points (\sweRobustFracHolm{} to \sweCompleteFracHolm{}), so the headline is not an artefact of the missing-log convention. The inclusion rule (at least 50 resolved instances) removes only \sweNExcluded{} of the \sweNFiles{} entries with a results file, so the results describe leaderboard rows that were publicly submitted and scored above that floor; they do not describe agents that were never submitted. The public repository changes over time; no snapshot hash was recorded when the files were downloaded (27 October 2026, from file timestamps). The latest commit touching \texttt{evaluation/verified} on the public repository, queried on 28 October 2026, is \texttt{92c5a4f} (1 September 2026), so the data are consistent with that state; this is an inference, not a verified identification. Many entries are best-of-$n$ or multi-sample systems and different scaffolds, budgets and dates are mixed; the comparison is of leaderboard rows, not of models.

\paragraph{$\tau$-bench.} The estimand is the difference between the two models' benchmark scores (mean over tasks of the per-task mean reward over four trials); the unit of analysis is the task. We chose as primary analysis, from the estimand and design and written into the analysis script before the comparison table was computed, the studentised sign-flip test of the task-level differences, with the task bootstrap for the interval and Holm adjustment over the two domains. The plain sign-flip test is exact only if the task differences are symmetric about zero; the studentised version has no finite-sample exactness under the weak null $E[d]=0$ and is supported by asymptotic arguments and by our skewness simulation (E1: $T\in\{20,50,100,300\}$, $R\in\{1,5\}$, skewness up to $5.5$, rejection rate at most \asymMaxSSF{}). The observed task differences are only mildly skewed (skewness \tauRetailSkew{} retail, \tauAirlineSkew{} airline), and every other procedure in Appendix~\ref{app:taubench} that targets the mean-score estimand gives the same conclusion, so the retail result does not hinge on this choice. For gpt-4o versus Claude 3.5 Sonnet the retail gap is \tauRetailDiff{} points (95\% task-bootstrap interval \tauRetailCI), primary $p=\tauPrimaryRetailP$ ($p=\tauPrimaryRetailHolm$ after Holm over the two domains); the plain sign-flip test gives $p=\tauRetailPPerm$ ($p=\tauRetailPHolm$ after Holm). The effect is detected in retail for the mean-score estimand, and every procedure in Appendix~\ref{app:taubench} that targets that estimand at the task level or with task clustering agrees. It is \emph{not} detected when the data are reduced to per-task majority outcomes: exact McNemar gives $p=\tauRetailPTaskMc$ when a two-of-four task counts as a failure and $p=\tauRetailMajTies$ when the \tauRetailNTies{} tied tasks are dropped. These tests address a different estimand (a majority-success difference of \tauRetailMajEst{} points instead of the mean-score difference), so the contrast shows that the conclusion depends on the representation of the data; it does not show that the majority test is conservative. McNemar tests on pass$^4$ and pass@4 give $p=\tauRetailPassHat$ and $p=\tauRetailPassAt$. The pooled-runs $z$-test ($p=\tauRetailPPool$) is invalid here and is listed only for contrast. In airline the gap is \tauAirlineDiff{} points \tauAirlineCI{} (a 20{,}000-draw bootstrap gives \tauAirlineBootLo{} to \tauAirlineBootHi{}: bootstrap endpoints move by up to half a point between seeds) with primary $p=\tauPrimaryAirlineP$ (pooled $p=\tauAirlinePPool$): not detected with \tauAirlineTasks{} tasks, which does not show that the two models are equal. Plug-in power calculations suggest about \tauAirlineNeed{} tasks would be needed at the observed airline gap (retail: \tauRetailNeed{}), but each is one draw from a very wide distribution: a task bootstrap of the pilot gives 95\% intervals of \tauAirlineNeedLo{}--\tauAirlineNeedHi{} (airline) and \tauRetailNeedLo{}--\tauRetailNeedHi{} (retail) tasks, so they are orders of magnitude, not predictions. Variance is dominated by run noise (\tauAirlineRunShare{} airline, \tauRetailRunShare{} retail) and task difficulty (\tauAirlineTaskShare, \tauRetailTaskShare), with agent-by-task interaction of \tauAirlineInterShare{} and \tauRetailInterShare.  Figure~\ref{fig:tau} contrasts pass@$k$ with pass$^k$: both models look strong when one success in $k$ suffices and much weaker when all $k$ must succeed. The data cover two models and two domains, and the model versions are those of the released trajectories.
